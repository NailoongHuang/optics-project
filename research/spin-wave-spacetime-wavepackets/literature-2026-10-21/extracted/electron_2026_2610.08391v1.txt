Space-Time Electron Wave Packets with Tunable Skyrmion Textures
Sharoz Schezwen
School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore
Yijie Shen∗
Centre of Disruptive Photonics Technologies, School of Physical and Mathematical Sciences, Singapore and
School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore
(Dated: October 7, 2026)
Space-Time Wave Packets (STWPs) have enabled numerous nontrivial properties, such as tunable
group velocities, nondiffraction and nonlocal spin textures in optical fields.
However, this has
not been properly studied in matter waves. Here, we investigate free-electron STWPs, which are
propagation-invariant and group-velocity-tunable matter wave packets, constructed by constraining
the spatiotemporal spectrum of the electron wavefunction in momentum space. Due to the unique
dispersion of free-electrons, the electron STWP can carry two different topologies, and host both
meron and skyrmion topological textures in momentum space.
We demonstrate the topological
phase transitions and tunable skyrmion numbers by spectral geometry tuning. We also show the
topologically robust propagation of the electron pulses and corresponding real-space skyrmionic
expectation value textures encoded within each single electron wavefunction. Our results open a
platform for topological free-electron wavepackets uses in sensing and information transfer.
Introduction - Structured free-electron waves have
emerged as a reliable and powerful platform to control
quantum matter waves, enabling the creation of elec-
tron vortex beams carrying angular momentum [1–4],
to shaped electron wavepackets used for microscopy and
spectroscopy [5–7].
Yet single free-electron wavepack-
ets remain fundamentally limited by issues such as the
diffractive spreading and dispersion, and temporal walk
off that limits the interaction lengths in free-electron-
photon interactions; persistent bottlenecks in electron
optics applications [8–10]. Beyond this, a free-electron
has an intrinsic spin degree of freedom, fundamentally
limiting the possibility of creating spin textures for sin-
gle electrons [11]. Such spin textures may provide fast
spin filters for measuring material spin textures from
the Hund’s exchange term in the interaction Hamilto-
nian [12, 13] (see Supplemental Material F), without the
need for complex vector reconstruction or sample tilting.
Recently, Space-Time Wave Packets (STWPs) are an
emerging class of structured waves with a correlated
space and time degrees of freedom, which gives the waves
a well defined and tunable group velocity and great re-
sistance against ordinary diffraction and dispersion [14–
31]. An optical STWP was also shown to carry a non-
diffracting meron, by using a metasurface to imprint a
polarization texture in momentum space [32], while re-
lated momentum space merons have also been realized
through photonic crystal slabs [33, 34].
The merons
are a half skyrmion, topologically nontrivial spin tex-
tures originally introduced in nuclear physics, which have
since been obtained in other fields [35–45]. The key fea-
ture of a skyrmionic texture is the topological robust-
ness, making it attractive for robust transport and in-
formation encoding [46–48]. In optical STWP, the fun-
damental structure of the light cone makes the meron

Results - To construct an electron STWP, the free-
electron dispersion relation is given by the solution to
the Schrodinger equation in a closed system, written as
ω = ℏk2/2m, where k2 and m is the momentum and
the mass of the elementary electron.
Throughout this
paper, both ℏand m is taken to be 1 for the analytical
calculations. A spectral constraint in the form of a plane
(ω = ω0+tan θ(kz −k0)) intersects the dispersion surface
at an angle θ, giving a well defined group velocity of the
electron wavepacket (Figure 1a). The intersection curve,
called the spectral support curve, can be projected onto
the kx −ω plane (Figure 1b), which shares at least one
point (k0, ω0) with the spectral plane. We call this lowest
frequency ω0 the carrier frequency, as seen in the kz −ω
plane (Figure 1c). Tuning the parameters k0 and θ (the
tilt of the spectral plane) gives different spectral projec-
tions onto the plane kx −ω and kz −ω (Figure 1d,e,f).
In this paper, we focus on the superluminal electrons
with group velocities above the carrier group velocity at
(k0, ω0).
These projections form the energy-momentum rela-
tions that the electron plane waves should possess to form
a single electron STWP. Upon introducing the dimension
ky, the curve in Figures 1b,e is revolved into a three-
dimensional surface in momentum space. The different
topologies of the three dimensional surfaces, either closed
or open, is used to form momentum space skyrmions and
fractional skyrmions respectively. This is due to the mo-
mentum space surface covering a full Bloch sphere, or a
portion of the Bloch sphere. This will be detailed more
below. We first give an analytic description of the range
where momentum space merons and skyrmions can be
hosted in an electron STWP based on the three dimen-
sional topology of the spectral surface and for a given
maximum electron energy with frequency ωmax.
To investigate the topology, the value of the carrier
wavenumber and the group velocity was used as free pa-
rameters. First, by imposing a condition where the spec-
tral plane intersects the dispersion surface at exactly the
carrier frequency, the lowest group velocity where the
electron wavepackets has to possess to get a closed mo-
mentum space surface is obtained.

to the gradient of the dispersion surface ∂ω/∂kz at the
point (k0, ω0). The group velocity has to satisfy Equa-

Conclusion - In summary, we demonstrate that free
space propagating STWPs can be created using non-
relativistic free-electron matter waves, and the electron
spinor can be used to construct propagation invariant
momentum space topological textures tunable by the
group velocity and the carrier wavenumber.
At high
group velocities, the momentum space surface will only
host merons, as there is no closed momentum space sur-
face directly obtained through the intersection of the
spectral plane and the dispersion surface. However, at
lower group velocities, the parabolic nature of the free-
electron dispersion naturally allows a closed sphere in
(kx, ky, ω) space. The closed momentum space structure
allows the construction of momentum space skyrmions,
and forms an analogous Bloch sphere.
By this particular tuning, we show the possibility to
generate persistent skyrmionic SEV structures in real-
space that exhibits a helicity phase shift of π/2.
This paper reveals a new class of free-space wave packet
with a topological wavefunction and charge, whose topol-
ogy is independently tunable without material band-
structures. As this topological information exists in real-
space, this may be a new robust route to transporting
information with matter and for sensing applications.
Another property automatically inherited from the
Space-Time Wave Packets structure is self healing [51],
which allows the skyrmions and meron SEV proposed
here to propagate for large distances even with small
perturbations. This approach provides a bridge between
STWP and structured matter waves, and the proposed
system may also offer a platform for studying tunable
topological textures in matter waves beyond conventional
methods typically done through Bose-Einstein Conden-
sates [52–56]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the
first single electron wavepacket free space propagating
skyrmion, shown via the local SEV of electrons, and we
assert this is still a single electron state. Our work opens
up a range of possibilities of integration with a large
body of existing literature on optical skyrmions, and
free-electron behaviour. Such electron skyrmionic wave
packets may be relevant to free-electron quantum optics,
free-electron-photon interactions, may provide additional
degrees of freedom for free-electron entanglement, mi-
croscopy, free-electron-matter interaction and transition
radiation and light generation by free-electrons [57–63],
as well as any future schemes for information encoding
and transfer that will empower future technologies.
