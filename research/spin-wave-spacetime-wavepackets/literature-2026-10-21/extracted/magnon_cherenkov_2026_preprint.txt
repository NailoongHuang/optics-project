Abstract
Radiation from an uniformly moving source, referred to as the Cherenkov eﬀect, is a universal phe-
nomenon, which enables emission of waves and ﬁnds important applications in various areas of physics
from particle physics to plasmonics and beyond. Currently, unveiling the potential of Cherenkov
emission of coherent spin waves, or magnons, is hampered by a lack of experimentally implemented
fast-moving magnetic perturbations. In this work, we uncover the magnon-Cherenkov eﬀect demon-
strating experimentally the emission of the exchange spin waves enabled by an optically induced
picosecond strain pulse, which acts as a spatially localized propagating perturbation of the internal
eﬀective magnetic ﬁeld as a result of magnetoelastic coupling. Through time-resolved measurements
and micromagnetoelastic simulations, we reveal the sagittal propagation of a strain pulse in a dielec-
tric ferrimagnet followed by the emitted SWs fully in line with the conditions of the Cherenkov eﬀect.
The spectral characteristics of the emitted spin waves are controlled with an applied magnetic ﬁeld
and the shape of the strain pulse itself. Establishing a picosecond strain pulse as a source for the
magnon-Cherenkov eﬀect signiﬁcantly expands the possibilities to realize and control non-dissipative
spin transport in various laterally and vertically structured magnonic devices.
Keywords: Cherenkov eﬀect, spin waves, magnonics, picosecond acoustics
Magnonic devices use coherent spin waves (SWs) to encode, transfer, and process information [1]. Consid-
erable advancement has been made in recent years in the development of planar two-dimensional (2-D)
magnonic structures, featuring GHz magnetostatic waves that are guided laterally from their area of
origin [2]. As a result, magnonic elements with diverse functionalities are actively being developed for
analog and digital computing [3] with a potential to outperform conventional electronic-based schemes in
terms of scaling down, operating frequencies and power consumption [4, 5]. In addition to the implemen-
tation of classical binary logic operations [6, 7], non-conventional computation paradigms computation
paradigms [8], including magnonic neuromorphic computing [9] are emerging. Scaling down, bursting oper-
ation frequencies, and increasing the density of magnonic elements demand means to generate SWs

beyond the magnetostatic regime, i.e. possessing short wavelengths and high velocities, with low damp-
ing, and propagating in any lateral or sagittal direction in magnetic heterostructures [10]. Currently, the
two main paradigms to excite coherent SWs exploit either localized alternating magnetic ﬁeld or spin
current, or propagating acoustic waves coupled to SWs. However, as waves of any nature, coherent SWs
can be generated via the Cherenkov mechanism, which is the fundamental process of wave emission from
a uniformly moving and spatially localized source [11]. The breakthrough discovery of electromagnetic
wave emission from uniformly moving charged particles by Pavel Cherenkov [12] was awarded the Nobel
Prize in 1958, and the Cherenkov eﬀect has been investigated in multiple ﬁelds of physics and beyond:
particle physics [13], plasmonics [14] and biomedicine [15]. The potential of this mechanism for magnon-
ics lies in the accessibility of the exchange SWs and the possibility of guiding them by controlling the
direction of propagation of the source, which requires the search for a suitable moving perturbation of
the magnetic media [16].
In analogy to the Cherenkov eﬀect for electromagnetic and sound waves, such an emission of SWs
requires the perturbation of a magnetic medium which moves with a velocity matching the SW phase
velocity Vph and is suﬃciently localized in the direction of propagation. In a one-dimensional scenario,
an inﬁnitely localized magnetic ﬁeld pulse of strength H0 exempliﬁes the concept of Cherenkov emission
of SWs. This pulse propagates through a magnetically ordered medium along the z axis at the velocity
V , and is represented by the Dirac delta function H(t, z) = H0δ(z −V t), where t is time (Fig. 1a). The
impact of the ﬁeld on magnetic moments is described by the torque T(z, t) = T0δ(z −tV ), where T0 is its
magnitude. This spatially localized torque is known to trigger SWs propagating in the ±z direction with
the distribution of wavenumbers k deﬁned by the Fourier transform (FT) of the torque T(z, t) [17–19].
Thus, the Dirac delta torque enables the generation of SWs with any k when considering that T(z) is
ﬁxed in space, and the corresponding frequencies ω are given by the SW dispersion relation ω(k). When
the pulse moves through a media, 2-D FT of the torque ˆδ(kV + ω) yields that only SWs satisfying the
velocity matching (VM) condition will be generated:

The torque of a ﬁnite spatial size and an arbitrary shape imposes a constraint on the amplitudes of
the SWs depending on their wavenumbers [20]. When this torque is moving, SWs emitted due to the
Cherenkov eﬀect are determined by the VM-condition with additional restriction given by its spectrum

Thus, to realize Cherenkov emission of SWs, a source producing a spatially localized torque with a
required spectrum and moving with the SW phase velocity is required.
In Ref. [21], an experimental realization of the magnon-Cherenkov eﬀect is demonstrated with
magnetic-ﬁeld ﬂux quanta produced by moving Abrikosov vortices in a superconductor/ferromagnet struc-
ture, which serve as moving localized magnetic ﬁeld pulses. This experiment conﬁrms the excitation of
monochromatic exchange SWs with wavelengths as short as 35 nm at cryogenic temperatures. Neverthe-
less, the problem remains of an implementation of a fast-moving localized ﬁeld at room temperature with
the possibility to propagate in various directions and controllable spectral characteristics. A perspective
candidate for a propagating torque is a moving domain wall [22, 23], which acts locally on a magneti-
zation, and an extremely narrow wall can be considered in the same way as the moving Dirac pulse
with the VM-condition deﬁned by equation (1). However, the Walker breakdown limits the propagation
velocity of domain walls in ﬂat structures, and an experimental demonstration is challenging and yet to
be realized. Here, magnetic nanotubes are a promising platform to overcome the speed limit [24]. Laser
pulses are known to produce eﬀective magnetic ﬁeld pulses, i.e. a perturbation of internal ﬁelds, via var-
ious femtomagnetic processes [25, 26]. However, with the large group and phase velocities, such pulses
satisfy the VM-condition only at very low k, resulting mostly in the excitation of the uniform precession
of magnetization.

In this work, we exploit the propagating eﬀective ﬁeld provided by a bipolar picosecond strain pulse,
which can be implemented at room temperature in various media [27]. Consider a mechanical displace-
ment of atoms uz(z, t) in the form of a Gaussian function moving with a sound velocity, which induces
an eﬀective ﬁeld through a perturbation of the magnetoelastic energy and the associated torque T(z)
(Fig. 1a). In general, the dispersion curves of the exchange SWs and acoustic phonons have two inter-
section points, marked with the brown points in Fig. 1b,c, where the VM-condition (1) is satisﬁed, and
the Cherenkov emission of the SWs is expected to occur. In addition, the amplitude of the emitted SWs
is given by the Fourier amplitude at a particular kI,II, which is controlled by the spatial length of the
displacement pulse (Fig, 1b). The signiﬁcant diﬀerence in the group velocities of SWs at the intersec-
tion points of the dispersion curves results in the propagation of the SWs with kI and kII behind and
ahead of the strain pulse, respectively, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 1a. In the following, we exper-
imentally uncover the magnon-Cherenkov eﬀect resulting in the emission of the exchange SWs from the
picosecond strain pulse propagating sagittally in iron garnet and support the experimental results with
micromagnetoelastic simulations.
Observation of Cherenkov emission of spin waves
We explore the Cherenkov emission of SWs on the platform of the 1.74-µm-thick ﬁlm of lutetium iron gar-
net (LuBi)3(FeGa)5O12 (LuIG) grown on the gadolinium gallium garnet (GGG) substrate. The sample
was partially covered with a 100-nm-thick gold layer (Au) that serves as an optoacoustic transducer [28].
Linearly polarized femtosecond laser pump pulses with the central wavelength of 515 nm are focused
either onto the Au layer to generate a sagittally propagating strain pulse via a thermoelastic mechanism
or onto the uncovered LuIG ﬁlm to directly excite magnetization dynamics (see Fig. 2a and Methods).
The time-resolved response is detected at the opposite interface of the LuIG ﬁlm as a change of inten-
sity or polarization rotation of the 450 nm probe pulses reﬂected from the LuIG/GGG interface. The
central wavelengths of the pump and probe pulses guarantee their spatial separation in LuIG, since the
penetration depths of both pulses are below 100 nm.
To verify that the strain pulse is indeed generated in the structure, we ﬁrst measure pump-induced
changes in the reﬂectivity. The resulting time trace consists of high-frequency oscillations with increas-
ing amplitude, which emerge slightly after the pump pulse impact on the Au/LuIG (Fig. 2b). Then, the
oscillations experience an abrupt change at t ≈0.26 ns, when the frequency is reduced and the enve-
lope acquires a complex shape (see inset of Fig. 2b). The fast Fourier transform (FFT) spectrum of the
entire time trace comprises three main frequency peaks (dark blue bottom spectrum in Fig. 2c): the low-
frequency group of peaks around 13 GHz, the narrow peak at 55.2 GHz and the broad peak at 69.4 GHz.
The sagittal propagation of the acoustic pulse results in a time-domain Brillouin scattering (TDBS) and
corresponding oscillations in the measured time traces. The mechanism behind the TDBS oscillations lies
in the interference of the probe pulse reﬂected from the LuIG/GGG interface with that reﬂected from the
propagating strain pulse [29]. We observe that the time trace before t = 0.26 ns reveals the only TDBS
frequency 69.4 GHz (gray middle spectrum in Fig. 2c) corresponding to the strain pulse propagating in
the LuIG with the velocity 6.55 km s−1 of the longitudinal acoustic (LA) phonons [30]. The rest of the
time trace consists of the oscillations with the TDBS frequency 55.2 GHz originating from the strain pulse
in GGG with the LA sound velocity of 6.2 km s−1 along with the low-frequency group of peaks (light blue
top spectrum in Fig. 2c). Therefore, the strain pulse is produced on the Au/LuIG side of the sample and
propagates to the LuIG/GGG interface. To identify the dynamics corresponding to the low-frequency
peaks, we study the transient magneto-optical response.
In the probe polarization rotation measurements, when the pump pulse is focused onto the Au/LuIG,
the magneto-optical response is identiﬁed as oscillations with an amplitude and frequency dependent
on the external magnetic ﬁeld. Such oscillations occur at time delays exceeding ∆t ≈0.26 ns, i.e. the
magnetization dynamics follows the strain pulse (blue line in Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. E1). In
contrast, direct excitation of the uncovered LuIG ﬁlm yields the excitation of the magnetization dynamics
right after the pump impact (red line in Fig. 2d). The FFT of these time traces reveals that the two main

Micromagnetoelastic simulations
We support our interpretation of the experimental results as the strain pulse-driven Magnon-Cherenkov
eﬀect with micromagnetoelastic simulations. We numerically solved the system of coupled Landau-
Lifshitz-Gilbert equation for magnetization M(r, t) and the elastodynamic equation (Navier-Cauchy) for
mechanical displacement u(r, t). The strain pulse is given a Gaussian proﬁle uz(z, t) with a total duration
of 20 ps and injected into LuIG, and the magnetization dynamics in response to the pulse propagation is
calculated. Details of the numerical methods are presented in Ref. [32] and Methods.
A snapshot of the spatial distribution of the out-of-plane magnetization component over the sagittal
direction Mz(z), the displacement uz(z) and the strain tensor component εzz(z) at t = 141 ps are shown in
Fig. 4a and d. It is seen that there are long-(short-)wavelength SW propagates behind (ahead) the strain
pulse. These SWs are continuously emitted as the strain pulse traverses the ﬁlm (see Supplementary Movie
1). The probe pulse with penetration depth δpr = 75 nm is sensitive to a small portion of the magnetization
dynamics averaged over a narrow region near the LuIG/GGG interface, thus limiting the wavenumber
resolution (see gray area in Fig. 4a and Methods). Therefore, SWs with wavelengths shorter than 186 nm
(k larger than 33.7 rad µm−1) cannot be resolved in the experiments, justifying why SWs with frequency
fII are not present in the experimental data. We employ spatial averaging with exponentially decaying
weights to obtain Mz(t) time trace to account for the penetration depth available in the experiment.

the injection of the strain pulse. Its FFT spectrum consists of the large peak corresponding to the exchange

Discussion
The group velocities of the emitted SWs are in the range between 1.2 and 2.2 km s−1 for kI and between
10.9 and 11.9 km s−1 for kII, which diﬀer signiﬁcantly from the strain pulse velocity (Fig. 1c) and lead to
SWs propagation behind and ahead of the strain pulse (Fig. 4a,d). However, SW with kII, propagating
ahead of the strain, is not observed in the experiments. Although the VM-condition is satisﬁed for both
intersection points, equation (2) yields the amplitude condition on the magnon-Cherenkov eﬀect, which is

we obtain that the SW wavenumber should be in the range between 0.06σ−1 and 2.76σ−1. For the 20 ps
long strain pulse (σ = 16 nm), this range is from 4 to 170 rad µm−1. Evidently, for longer strain pulses,
the range of accessible SW wavenumbers reduces and shifts toward the lower k. We numerically conﬁrm
this showing the absence of SW with kII and the increased amplitude of SW with kI during the Cherenkov
emission from the strain pulse with a duration of 70 ps (Extended Data Fig. E2 and Supplementary movie
2). This is fully consistent with conservation laws of momentum and energy for the magnon-phonon
system, which also implies a modiﬁcation of the initial spectrum of the strain pulse by emitted SWs.
Consequently, the strain pulse acquires a small distortion during propagation. At the same time, the 2-D
FFT of the spatio-temporal evolution of uz(z, t) shows that the phonon dispersion remains linear (see
Fig. 4e), indicating an absence of a strong hybridization between the emitted SWs and the strain pulse.
Since the Cherenkov emission accompanies the strain pulse during its propagation, we attempted to
detect these SWs using probe pulses with 900 nm central wavelength. Since the studied LuIG sample is
almost transparent at 900 nm, such probe pulses are sensitive to the magnetization dynamics averaged
over the entire sample thickness. When the pump pulse is focused directly onto the LuIG, only the
magnetization dynamics at the FMR frequency f0 is detected. Under the excitation of the Au/LuIG the
magnetization dynamics is detected without a time delay (Extended Data Fig. E4). The FFT spectra of
this dynamics show, in addition to the FMR mode, the presence of spin waves generated through the
Cherenkov eﬀect. Moreover, distinct frequency combs appear in the spectra, arising from the strain pulse
and corresponding SWs reﬂecting multiple times between the sample interfaces.
In the outlook, we discuss the possible impact of the demonstrated magnon-Cherenkov eﬀect on the
development of magnonics. Unlike other propagating SW sources, such as domain walls, vortices, and
even light pulses, strain pulses generally have favorable propagation properties in various media and
heterostructures. The magnon-Cherenkov eﬀect therefore enables generation of SWs from strain pulses
moving within magnetic/magnetic or magnetic/nonmagnetic multilayers, as well as in patterned struc-
tures [31, 33, 34]. Here, SWs in diﬀerent constituents remain coherent but exhibit distinct characteristics
determined by the speciﬁc magnetic and acoustic properties of the layers involved. Thus, complex mag-
netic dynamics, controlled by external magnetic ﬁelds, could ﬁnd applications in reservoir computing [35].
On the other hand, one could envisage multilayer or patterned structures with a large acoustic impedance
mismatch at a particular interface, thus making it possible to inject SW without injection of the strain
pulse through the interface, or even through an air spacer [36]. This makes it possible to physically decou-
ple the source of an exchange SW and a waveguide. By designing optoacoustic transducers, sequences of
strain pulses with controlled distance can be obtained and utilized for a unidirectional Cherenkov emission
of SW in analogy to the numerical concept proposed in [37]. Regarding the development of approaches to
realize Cherenkov emission of a SW, we note that in heterostructures, such as magnonic crystals [38] and
in hybrid magnon-phonon crystals [39], the velocity threshold can be modiﬁed in an analogy to Cherenkov
electromagnetic radiation by a charged particle moving in a photonic crystal [40]. An intriguing possi-
bility to generate SW by acoustic solitons [27] has yet to be explored to access exceptionally high SW
wavenumbers owing to the strong localization of mechanical displacements to be utilized as, e.g. sources
of THz electromagnetic waves [41]. Finally, as the SWs excitation is possible through dynamical perturba-
tions of exchange, spin-orbital, and magneto-dipolar eﬀective ﬁelds, mechanisms of SW generation from
a strain pulse beyond magnetoelastic coupling can be explored.
Methods
Sample
The sample is a single-crystal ﬁlm (LuBi)3(FeGa)5O12 with a thickness of 1.74 µm, grown by a liquid-
phase epitaxy method on (110) gadolinium gallium garnet (GGG) substrate. The surface of the sample
was covered with a 100-nm-thick gold layer using a thermal evaporation process. The 256 × 256 µm2
windows in gold are made using electron beam lithography combined with reactive ion etching by an
Ar-ion plasma.

The magnetic parameters of the sample were characterized using the Smit-Beljers approach [42] in
combination with the pump-probe FMR frequency measurements. We performed FFT of time traces at
diﬀerent azimuthal directions of the magnetic ﬁeld with µ0Hext = 415 mT (Extended Data Fig. E5), when
the pump pulses are focused onto the Au/LuIG and the probe pulses central wavelength of 1030 nm.
The free energy density for substituted iron garnets grown on the (110)-type substrates is written in a
form [43]:

terms are expressed as follows:

are the in-plane components of the external magnetic ﬁeld Hext; Ku, Ki and Kxz are the uniaxial out-
of-plane, in-plane and orthorhombic anisotropy parameters, respectively. Writing (5) in the spherical
coordinates allows a numerical calculation of the FMR frequency, knowing the equilibrium direction of
M, as described elsewhere [42, 44]. By ﬁtting the azimuthal and ﬁeld dependence of FMR frequency
(solid red line in Extended Data Fig. E5 and red dashed line in Fig. 3a) we acquire the following magnetic
parameters of the sample: Ms = 13.5 kA m−1, Ku = 194 J m−3, Ki = 449 J m−3 and Kxz = 325 J m−3.
Pump-probe experiment details
We used the Yb:KGW regenerative ampliﬁer laser system (PHAROS, Light Conversion) as a source of
laser pulses with a duration of 170 fs, central wavelength of 1030 nm, 5 kHz repetition rate, and pulse
energy up to 1 mJ. We split the laser pulses into two parts. The ﬁrst part was used as a linearly polar-
ized pump pulse with a central wavelength of 515 nm after frequency doubling with β-barium borate
(BBO) crystal. The second part of the initial pulses was used to pump the optical parametric ampliﬁer
(ORPHEUS, Light Conversion), which produced linearly polarized pulses with a duration of 150 fs and a
central wavelength of 900 nm. We used the second BBO crystal to achieve the probe pulses with a central
wavelength of 450 nm.
The pump pulses after passing a mechanical delay line were focused onto the sample in a spot with a
diameter of 50 µm with typical ﬂuence of 50 mJ cm−2. The probe pulses were focused from the opposite
side through an optically transparent GGG substrate in a spot with a diameter of 25 µm and a ﬂuence
of at least 10 times less than for the pump pulses. An angle of incidence of probe pulses was 17◦, and a
spatial ﬁlter was used on the reﬂected beams. The scheme allows distinguishing the probe pulses reﬂected
from the diﬀerent sample interfaces and, therefore, to exclude parasitic back-reﬂection from the substrate.
Pump-induced rotation of the probe’s polarization was measured using the balanced photodetector (New
Focus 2107, Newport) in combination with the Wollaston prism and half-wave plate. The signal from
the detector was demodulated by the lock-in ampliﬁer (SR830, Stanford Research Systems) synchronized
with the optical chopper placed in the pump pulse optical path. The sample was placed inside the
electromagnet to apply the external magnetic ﬁeld in the sample’s plane. All experiments were carried
out at room temperature.

Probe laser pulses sensitivity
The probe pulse of 150 fs duration sets an upper frequency limitation of an order of THz and does not
obscure the detection of SWs of interest. Detection of the propagating SW through the Bragg mechanism
used in Ref.[45] requires the matching between the SW and the light wavevectors and is not applicable
in our case. Indeed, the probe pulse of 450 nm is sensitive to the SW with k = 66 rad µm−1, and SW
frequency lies between f = 44.5 GHz and f = 52.8 GHz at used Hext, which is not observed in our
experiments. However, the strongly decaying 450 nm probe intensity proﬁle in the sample itself enables
the detection of sagittally propagating magnetization disturbances. With the FT of the exponential depth
proﬁle of the probe intensity, we obtain the spectral amplitude S of SW with particular k in the detected
signal, which scales with the probe penetration depth δpr as:
S(k) =
1

1 + (kδpr)2 .
(7)
Thus, the use of the 450 nm probe pulses with δpr = 75 nm enables eﬃcient detection of SWs with k up to
33.7 rad µm−1 determined as S(k) = e−1 in equation (7), which corresponds to the wavelength of 186 nm
(value S(k) is color-coded background in Fig.3b). The frequency of SW detected in the experiments fI,
according to equation (3) corresponds to the wavenumbers kI in the range from 12.6 to 23.3 rad µm−1. SW
with these wavenumbers appear to be detected eﬃciently, while fII SW with kII from 116 to 127 rad µm−1
are not resolved.

calculations, a Gilbert damping value α = 10−3 was used. We assigned the elastic damping parameter β
